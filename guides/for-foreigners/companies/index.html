Guides · Cyprus tax for foreigners in 2026

A Cyprus company and the 15% corporate tax

From 2026 the Tax Department publishes corporate tax at 15%. Deemed distribution on profits from 2026 is abolished. A dividend to a domiciled individual is at 5%.

Figures on this site are taken from the official and primary sources linked on each page and describe the position as published for 2026. They are general information, not legal, tax or investment advice, and not a quote from the Land Registry or the Tax Department. Rules, reliefs and local fees change, and a transaction can fall outside the ordinary case. Check the linked source, and take advice on your own facts, before you rely on a result.

Last checked: 26 November 2026.

Short answer

On the company-return page the Tax Department publishes a rate table. From 2026 corporate tax is 15%. For 2013–2025 the same table puts 12.5%. For 2003–2012 it puts 10%. That is the rate on company profit that falls inside the statute. It is not the rate on the owner’s salary, and it is not a promise that a foreigner’s whole life moves into a company.

The dividend after the tax

The Department’s defence-contribution page, from 2026, puts dividends at 5%. The Ministry of Finance incentives page says the same: the contribution is withheld on dividends received by individuals at 5% from profits earned after 1 January 2026, and before that date at 17%.

The Department’s note states the grandfathering. A dividend received from a resident company by 31 December 2031 out of profits of tax years up to and including 2025 stays at 17%. The example on that note is a 2027 dividend out of 2025 profits at 17%, and out of 2026 profits at 5%.

A person who is not domiciled is outside the contribution on dividends entirely. That is the Department’s residency page, not a discount bought in year one. The conditions are in the non-dom guide.

Deemed dividend distribution on profits from 2026 onwards is marked abolished on the Department’s page. The transitional rules for earlier profits, in the same note, are long. This page does not compress them into one figure.

Separate rows of the same table concern a dividend to a company in a non-cooperative jurisdiction (17%) and a hidden distribution (10%). They are not the ordinary rate for an individual shareholder.

What a comparison with self-employment leaves out

The calculator applies 15% and, if the whole after-tax profit is paid as a dividend out of 2026 profits, contribution at 5% or at nil. Beside it is income tax on the same profit under the 2026 bands, as if it were self-employed income.

Social insurance, GHS, expenses, a director’s salary and the employment exemptions are not in the figures. The test under which a company is tax resident merely because it is incorporated in Cyprus is not restated here: Article 2 was not quoted line by line from the consolidation on this date. Where a treaty says otherwise, the treaty is what is read. The list is on the treaty page.

This page does not state a separate contribution rate on a company’s interest, beyond the rows the Department’s table prints in terms, such as 17% on a payment into a non-cooperative jurisdiction. The general sentence “all company interest is only in corporate tax”, taken from a secondary summary, is not brought over.

Questions

Is 15% the tax on the dividend as well?

15% is corporate tax on the profit. A dividend to a domiciled individual out of 2026 profits is charged defence contribution at a further 5%. A person who is not domiciled does not pay that contribution.

Is an old dividend out of 2024 profits still at 17%?

The Department’s note says a dividend from a resident company out of profits up to 2025, received by 31 December 2031, stays at 17%. That is not the rate on 2026 profits.

Does the calculator choose a company for me?

No. It does not compute social-insurance contributions and it does not decide whether a payment is a dividend. It illustrates two published rates.

Is deemed distribution still there?

The Department’s page says it was abolished for profits from 2026 onwards. The transition for earlier profits, in the same note, is not reduced to one sentence here.
